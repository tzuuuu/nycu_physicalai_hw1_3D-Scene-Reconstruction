1 Introduction
Imagine asking a household robot to bring you something from the kitchen. To do that safely, it needs a
useful picture of the home: where rooms and furniture are, how spaces connect, and where it can move
without collisions. A 3D scene map provides a spatial representation of the environment that can support
navigation and scene understanding [ 1].
Figure 1: Visual pipeline from RGB-D observations to a 3D scene.
In this assignment, you will reconstruct apartment_0, an indoor scene from the Replica dataset [ 2],
using RGB-D (color and depth) observations in Habitat-Sim [ 3] simulator. The reconstruction pipeline
converts depth images into 3D point clouds, registers observations from multiple viewpoints, integrates
them into a unified 3D scene, and evaluates the result through visual inspection and comparison of the
estimated camera trajectory with ground truth. In addition to reconstruction, you will examine the input
observations to understand how conditions such as missing or noisy depth, insuﬀicient viewpoint overlap,
and large frame-to-frame motion relate to the resulting map and trajectory. Together, these components
connect the characteristics of the input data with the behavior of the reconstruction process. Although
conducted in simulation, this assignment provides a starting point for considering the challenges involved
in building and using 3D scene maps for real-world robotic tasks.
By completing this assignment, you will be able to:
1. Understand the pipeline from RGB-D observations to 3D scene reconstruction, including the pinhole
camera model, point-cloud generation, registration, mapping, and trajectory estimation.
2. Analyze input observations and capture choices to identify factors associated with reconstruction
behavior and failure cases.

2 Structure and Workflow
This assignment is organized into two phases that share the same reconstruction and data-verification
workflow. The main difference lies in how the input observations are obtained:
• Phase 1: Use a provided first-floor batch containing designed data-quality variations to reconstruct a
3D scene map, diagnose factors that contribute to reconstruction failures, and refine the reconstruction
based on the analysis.
• Phase 2: Extend the same workflow to the second floor, where you will collect your own RGB-D
observations and camera poses through navigation in the simulation and reconstruct the scene from
the collected data.
For the reconstruction component, the workflow follows the general registration procedure described
in the Open3D [4] documentation, including point-cloud downsampling, global registration, and local
refinement. The results from both phases are then compared to examine how data-collection choices and
observation characteristics relate to reconstruction outcomes.
2.1 3D Scene Map Reconstruction
2.1.1 Reconstruction Workflow
The reconstruction work in both phases follows the pipeline below:
1. Depth Unprojection. Convert valid depth pixels to 3D points using the pinhole camera model. Use
the resolution, field of view (FOV), and depth scale recorded in intrinsics.json. Implement this
mapping yourself; do not use Open3D projection functions for this step.
2. Voxelization (Downsampling). Reduce the number of points to improve computational eﬀiciency
while retaining the scene structure. Open3D functions may be used for this step[ 4].
3. Global Registration (RANSAC). Estimate an initial alignment between point clouds to handle large
viewpoint differences using the Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) [5]. Open3D functions may
be used for this step [ 4].
4. Local Registration (ICP). Starting from the global-registration result, refine the transformation be-
tween point clouds using the Iterative Closest Point (ICP) [ 6] algorithm. Open3D’s point-to-point or
point-to-plane ICP implementation may be used for this step [ 4].
5. Scene Map Reconstruction and Evaluation. Transform and accumulate the aligned point clouds
into a unified 3D scene map and construct the estimated camera trajectory. For clearer visualization,
remove the ceiling when necessary, and visualize the estimated trajectory in red and the ground-truth
trajectory in black. Evaluate the estimated trajectory against the ground truth using the required
Mean L2 Distance. Open3D functions may be used for point-cloud processing, transformation, and
visualization [4].
After completing the reconstruction, evaluate the resulting scene and camera trajectory together with
the data-verification process. If the reconstruction contains noticeable artifacts or alignment failures,
identify likely contributing factors from the observations and reconstruction settings, adjust the relevant
preprocessing or registration parameters, and rerun the pipeline as needed.

2.1.3 Global and Local Registration
Each point cloud is expressed in its own camera coordinate frame, so observations from different view-
points cannot be directly combined. Registration estimates the relative transformations needed to align
them in a shared coordinate system. Following the steps in Open3D document [ 4], the alignment is then
performed in two stages: global registration with Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) [ 5] algorithm,
and local refinement with Iterative Closest Point (ICP) [ 6] algorithm.
Global Registration. Global registration estimates an initial alignment between point clouds without
requiring a close initial pose. The point clouds are first downsampled and represented using Fast Point
Feature Histograms (FPFH) [ 8], which provide local geometric descriptors for establishing candidate
correspondences. Random Sample Consensus (RANSAC) [ 5] then repeatedly samples candidate corre-
spondences, estimates a transformation, and rejects inconsistent matches using geometric consistency
checks. Only correspondences that pass these checks are used to estimate and validate the transforma-
tion. The resulting alignment provides a coarse global registration that can be further refined in the
local-registration stage.
Local Registration. Local registration further refines the alignment between point clouds using Iterative
Closest Point (ICP) [ 6]. Starting from an initial transformation, ICP repeatedly establishes correspon-
dences between the source and target point clouds, estimates a transformation that reduces the alignment
error, and updates the source point cloud. This process continues until the convergence criterion is
reached. Open3D provides both point-to-point and point-to-plane formulations for ICP [ 4]. Point-to-
point ICP minimizes the Euclidean distance between corresponding points, whereas point-to-plane ICP
minimizes the distance from each source point to the tangent plane defined by the corresponding target
point and its surface normal.
After global and local registration, the estimated pairwise transformations describe the relative poses
between point clouds. By choosing one point cloud as the reference frame and composing these trans-
formations, the remaining point clouds can be transformed into the same coordinate system and accumu-
lated to form a complete scene map. Registration performance depends on parameters such as voxel size,
correspondence thresholds, and convergence criteria. Different settings may affect alignment quality,
robustness, runtime, and ultimately the reconstructed scene. These parameter choices should therefore
be examined and documented during the reconstruction process.

2.2 Data Verification
In Physical AI, a robot acts from time-varying sensor observations, so the quality of those observations
can directly affect downstream perception and especially in reconstruction. Missing depth, noisy mea-
surements, insuﬀicient geometric support, or abrupt camera motion may lead to alignment failures even
when the reconstruction method itself is unchanged. Data verification therefore helps identify and docu-
ment observation conditions before interpreting reconstruction results.
Raw images and numerical measurements do not, by themselves, describe how a data-quality obser-
vation relates to an experiment or a reconstruction run. This assignment uses a semantic layer to record
these relationships explicitly. An ontology [9] defines the shared concepts and relationships used to
describe capture batches, frames, quality factors, measurements, verification results, and reconstruction
runs.
2.2.1 Data Verification Workflow
The data-verification workflow connects the conditions observed in the input data with the reconstruction
results. It consists of the following stages:
• Experiment Declaration. Declare an experiment for a capture batch and specify any reconstruction-
setting adjustments. At this stage, no quality factors or qualification thresholds are selected.
• First-Pass Reconstruction. Run reconstruction on the declared capture while measuring all pre-
defined data-quality factors. These measurements provide an initial description of the conditions
observed throughout the sequence.
• Factor Recording. Record the raw factor measurements as experiment-scoped evidence. Measure-
ments are associated with individual frames or consecutive frame pairs, depending on the factor
definition. No qualification status or Pass/Fail decision is assigned.
• Data Inspection. Inspect the recorded measurements through summaries and visualizations of their
temporal behavior, distributions, and relationships. This allows the student to identify notable regions,
patterns, and possible tradeoffs in the captured data.
• Data Intervention and Reconstruction. Based on the observed evidence, formulate an intervention
intended to improve or probe reconstruction behavior. Data selection is one possible intervention,

where observations are retained or excluded according to discovered factor patterns. Other inter-
ventions may modify how the available data is used while preserving the recorded measurements as
unchanged evidence. Reconstruction is then repeated to evaluate the effect of the intervention.
• Experiment Reporting. Treat the sequence of experiment declarations, recorded factor measure-
ments, interventions, and reconstruction outcomes as a structured lab notebook. These records pro-
vide the evidence used to explain and compare reconstruction behavior across experiments.
The purpose of this workflow is not to classify observations as inherently good or bad before recon-
struction. Instead, it first records the observable data conditions, then uses those observations to motivate
and evaluate interventions. Data selection is therefore one possible response to the evidence rather than
the objective of the workflow itself. Different interventions can be compared using the same recorded
measurements and reconstruction procedure.
2.2.2 Data Quality Factors
The available factors are pipeline-grounded measurements: each factor records a first-order quantity
that is already produced or directly observed during the reconstruction process. Rather than introducing
separate image-quality heuristics, the factors expose intermediate states from point-cloud generation,
voxel downsampling, coarse registration, and local refinement.
The measurements are applied either to a single depth image or to an ordered pair of consecutive
depth images.
• Single-depth-image factors
– RawPointCount: the number of valid back-projected 3D points obtained from depth pixels with
positive depth values before voxel downsampling.
– VoxelizedPointYield: the number of points remaining after the reconstruction pipeline ap-
plies voxel downsampling. It describes how much geometric information survives the reduction
step.
• Consecutive depth-pair factors
– CoarseRANSACFitness: the correspondence inlier fraction reported by the coarse RANSAC
registration stage.
– CoarseRANSACRMSE: the root-mean-square error of the inlier correspondences produced by coarse
RANSAC registration.
– CoarseRANSACCorrespondences: the number of correspondences retained in the RANSAC
registration result.
– ICPInlierFitness: the correspondence inlier fraction reported by the ICP refinement stage.
– ICPInlierRMSE: the root-mean-square error of the inlier correspondences after ICP refinement.
– ICPInlierCorrespondences: the number of correspondences retained in the ICP registration
result.
These factors are diagnostic measurements of the reconstruction pipeline. Together, they expose how
much geometric support is available and how strongly consecutive observations agree during coarse and
local alignment. Students can inspect these measurements alongside reconstruction outcomes to identify
patterns, formulate hypotheses, and motivate subsequent data interventions.

first-pass reconstruction, inspects the resulting measurements through tables and plots, and uses the ob-

data selection or other changes to how the captured observations are used in reconstruction.
